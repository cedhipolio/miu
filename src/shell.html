How to use
Marking up a PDF, end to end
  • Open a PDF — the Open PDF button, or drop one anywhere on this page. Any PDF: a mount analysis, a drawing set, a scope, a submittal, a scan. Nothing is uploaded; the file is read in this browser and never leaves the machine. Where the tool recognises a report it names the export after it — a site code off the first page gives SOH3361 - Redlined Mount Analysis.pdf — and where it does not, the file keeps its own name. Either way, type over the name in the SITE box and the export follows.
  • Redline (R) covers a printed value with editable red text pre-filled with what is printed — for a model number or a part number that was simply installed differently. Strike & write (F) is the other pattern the reports use: it rules through the printed value and puts the as-built one on the line above, which is what centrelines and elevations get. Both leave you in the text, ready to type.
  • Callout (K) is for the drawing sheets. Pick a part in the panel's Parts tab and click what it points at: you get a styled box with the description, MFR, part number and how many per sector, and an arrow to the member. The library is seeded with the Site Pro 1, Sabre, CommScope and Ericsson parts these reports keep using, and anything you add stays for next time. The arrow is the anchor. Drag the box, or nudge it with the arrow keys, and the tip stays on the member it points at while the leader re-routes — including round to whichever side of the box now faces it. To move the whole callout, arrow and all, take it with a marquee (or Shift-click both halves) and drag that.
  • Markups keeps the graphics you use again and again — a north arrow, a detail, a photograph of what was actually installed. Upload once; from then on a click picks it up — a preview of it follows the pointer at the size it will land at, hanging from its top-left corner — and the next click on the sheet puts it down there. Escape puts it back, and Shift-click drops one in the middle of the page without the second click. It exports as a stamp annotation. The ✎ on a markup opens it in the picture editor: rub out the margin it came with, crop it, turn it or put white behind it, and every drop after that uses the tidied version. A markup already on a sheet is edited the same way, from its own Edit picture.
  • Notes writes one note across a run of pages — the same words in the same spot on each. Type it, choose the pages, then click where it goes. "ACTUAL INSTALL: …" and "STRESS RATIO IS < 1 …" are already in the list.
  • As-built takes the RFDS or the mount mapping for the same site, reads every model and centreline out of it, and lists the values in this report that it cannot account for. Each one is a button: Show me jumps to it, Redline it marks it. It finds them; you decide.
  • What you are drawing is what you get. Every tool shows the mark itself as you drag it — a line with its head, its weight and its pattern; a box or an ellipse in its own stroke, fill and hatch — rather than a rubber band you have to imagine. A selected mark keeps its handles even while you are typing in it, and every mark, stroke and shape carries the same three controls: ✥ to drag, ↻ to rotate and ✕ to delete.
  • Drawing on a sheet. Box (B) and Circle (O) drag out a shape you can also type inside — hold Ctrl while you drag for a perfect square or a true circle; Arrow (A) and Line (L) drag from one point to another; Polyline (P) takes a corner per click and finishes on a double-click or Enter; Text (E) drags out a text box. Everything they draw selects, moves, resizes, groups and undoes like any other mark, and a shape comes up selected rather than in text-editing, so Delete removes it and a double-click opens it for typing.
  • A text box holds what you type in it. It wraps to the width you drew and grows downwards to fit the lines, rather than hiding the ones past the bottom edge; delete a line and it gives the height back, down to the box you drew and no further. The width stays yours — drag the side handle to re-wrap it. Enter starts a new line. Narrowed, it fills each line to the edge, breaking inside a word where it runs out, the way a printed column does; the panel's Wrap chips put it back to breaking between words, and Vertical puts the block at the top, the middle or the foot of the box.
  • Nothing is outlined until it is picked. A mark that belongs to a group used to wear a ring all the time; now the ring means selected, and only that.
  • A tool in your hand draws, even over something. Drawing a box over a box you have already drawn draws the second box; it does not drag the first one out of the way. Select and Pan still pick a mark up, which is what those two are for. And a double-click reaches the text inside a grouped mark — a callout in a stamp opens for typing without ungrouping it.
  • Front and back cross the strokes. A mark sent to the front comes above every line on the page and one sent to the back goes under them, rather than only sorting against its neighbours in the same band. ⇅ is still the button that flips the band on its own.
  • A cloud round a member. Rectangle and Ellipse carry the stroke patterns under Rule pattern — cloud, wave, zigzag, sawtooth — so a revision cloud is the Box tool with one chip pressed, and it exports as the cloud it is rather than as a rectangle.
  • Copy properties paints what you point at. With the brush loaded, a plain click on a mark gives it the look; adding to a selection with Ctrl, or taking the whole page, is a selection and leaves the marks alone. Escape puts the brush down, and so does picking up any tool.
  • Snapshot comes back as a plain picture of the region, exactly as it is on the sheet. Its panel's Capture chips offer the other kind — the region converted to a redline of itself, page dropped out and ink recoloured — which is what lifting a detail off a drawing wants.
  • The laser is a continuous flare: a hot head with a streak behind it that fades from the tail, rather than a line of dots. Escape puts it away.
  • Every tool has its own properties. The ▾ in the lower-right corner of a tool's button opens them — a different panel for each tool, because a rectangle and a line have nothing in common to set. A right-click on the button opens the same panel. Rectangle and Ellipse have a border and a fill, an opacity for each, and a hatch with its own colour, scale and rotation. Line, Arrow and Polyline have a weight, a line style, an ending you can choose for each end at a tiny, small, medium or large tip size, and a pattern that ripples the run itself. Dashes are a fixed length — a dash is the same length at a hairline as it is at 6pt, so two rules of different weights on one sheet keep the same rhythm; you pick the rhythm instead, from fine, dashed, long and extra long, with dotted, dash-dot and dash-dot-dot beside them. Weights go down to a tenth of a point, and a hairline draws and exports as the hairline it is set to rather than being rounded up to something you did not ask for. Text and Callout have a size, a face, bold / italic / underline / strikethrough, alignment, and the two spacings — letter spacing in points and line spacing as a multiple of the normal pitch, both drawn the same way on screen and in the exported file. Everything has a highlight mode, under Advanced settings, that lets the drawing read through the mark. Each tool remembers its own, for good, in this browser: Box can stay blue at 2pt while Arrow stays red at 1.5pt. With something selected, a change in the panel restyles the selection too, so a box you have already drawn can change without being redrawn. A fill at 0% leaves a shape hollow, which is what you want over a drawing.
  • A tool you DRAW with stays in your hand — Box, Circle, Line, Arrow, Polyline, Text, Length and Callout — so you can place twenty without reaching for the button again. The tools that act on what is printed — Redline, Strike & write, Strike row, Strike text, Cross out, Copy row and Snapshot — put themselves down as soon as they have done it, and Select takes over, because the next click after one of those is nearly always "now select something". Escape puts any tool down and goes to Select, and so do S and the Select button. A single click on a mark selects it whatever tool is up; a double-click opens it for typing.
  • Snap and Magnet (in Arrange) are two different things and switch separately. Snap is about ANGLE: a stroke you draw and a mark you rotate settle on the nearest 15°, which is how a line comes out exactly level or plumb without being forced there. Magnet is about POSITION: a mark you move, resize, or whose stroke-point you drag is pulled onto the nearest edge, centre or baseline — of the other marks and of the printed page, so a correction lands on the column rule or the row it belongs to. Shift still forces a stroke level; holding Ctrl suspends either for one gesture.
  • Rotating says the angle. Drag the ↻ handle and the reading follows it, turning green the moment Snap catches a 15°. Shift forces 15° steps; Ctrl turns freely.
  • The bar along the bottom appears whenever anything is selected, and acts on all of it: edit properties, duplicate, layer (front, back, or behind the printed page), flatten (drawn into the page on export rather than added as an annotation), group, lock, save the selection to your markup library as a reusable stamp, apply it across a range of pages, copy, set a subject, align, and delete.
  • Each mark carries its own look, so one document can hold a red correction, a blue note and a green ring. The bar that appears on a selected mark adds two things the ribbon cannot: ▩ takes the ground off it, so the drawing reads through instead of being hidden, and ◐ gives that one mark everything the current tool's ▾ properties are set to. A selected stroke gets its own pair: ↗ cycles the arrowhead (end, both ends, none) and ◐ restyles it.
  • Read table (Ctrl+Shift+L) reads a table off the document and shows it as a form — every line as it is printed, blanks and all. Change a cell and press Redline the changes: only what you edited is written, each correction landing on the printed value it replaces. The chips above the table hide the columns you are not working on. On a mount analysis it finds the Antenna / Equipment Loading table wherever it is. On anything else — a construction drawing's schedule, a submittal's parts list — it reads the table on the page you are looking at, found by its shape rather than by its wording: the longest run of printed lines that hold the same values at the same places, under the line that names them. It is arithmetic on the text the PDF already carries; nothing is uploaded and no model is consulted, here or anywhere else in this tool.
  • Strike & write (F) rules through a printed value and writes the as-built one directly over it, on the same line and reading on top of the rule, which runs out either side of it. The writing carries its own white ground, so it covers the value it replaces and there is never a question which one that was — on a dense table a correction written on the line above reads as a correction to the row above. It stays on one line and on the middle of the rule as you type, and the rule grows with it.
  • Length (D) drags along whatever you want measured and leaves a dimension: a run with an arrow at each end and a reading written on it. The reading is yours to type — it comes up empty with the caret in it, and the length the run measures is shown as you drag it and in the status line, so you can type what it should say. Write in the measured length in its ▾ puts the measurement in for you, and from then on that reading follows the run until you type over it; the same panel sets the sheet's scale (the architect's and engineer's scales are listed), what the reading is written in, and — on a cropped or photocopied sheet — Calibrate from something whose length you know. The run and its reading are one mark, like a callout: click either to select the measurement, drag either to move the whole of it, and set the reading's own font, size and style under Reading. The reading sits above the middle of the run, clear of the rule, turned with it and always the right way up; it stays centred as you type it, and dragging it by its ✥ grip leaves it wherever you put it, keeping that offset as the run is moved.
  • Snapshot (Shift+S) has its own button in the ribbon. Drag a region and it comes back as a redline of itself, pasted at the same size, with no frame, and at the resolution of what you selected — the region is re-rendered from the PDF rather than cropped off the screen, so it stays sharp when you zoom in or export.
  • A snapshot can be edited. With one selected, Edit picture opens it on its own, zoomable (Fit, 1:1, ctrl-wheel) and with undo and redo. Erase is a continuous brush and Restore is its opposite — it brushes the original picture back where you rubbed too much out. The magic wand selects a space bounded by the picture’s own lines and can New / Add / Subtract (or hold Shift to add, Alt to subtract); the lasso takes whatever you draw round, through the same three chips, for everything the wand cannot reach; Select inverse swaps the selection for everything else, and Fill paints it — always underneath the redlines, so the drawing still reads on top of its new ground. White behind a captured drawing and nothing else is: wand the outside, invert, fill. Erase line takes a whole run of ink, end to end, from one click. Rotate left / right turns the picture itself a quarter at a time — losslessly, undoable, and the mark on the sheet takes the new proportions when you save; Free beside them turns it to any angle, previewing as you sweep and resampling once when you let go. Crop drags a box round what you want to keep and cuts everything outside it away; the mark shrinks with it, keeping the scale the picture already had, and Undo puts the margin back. The picture sits in the middle of the editor with its own edge and chequer drawn, so you can always see where it ends and which parts of it are transparent.
  • Strike row (W) rules through a whole row. Strike text (T) rules through one value. Cross out (X) drags a box over anything and leaves an X. Copy row (C) copies printed text as editable redlines on the line below. Click a stroke to select it, then drag any of its points.
  • Select (S): drag to move, drag a box over blank space to select several, Shift-click to add to the selection. Arrow keys nudge, Ctrl+D duplicates, Ctrl+G groups (Ctrl+Shift+G ungroups), Delete removes. Every tool has a letter — S select, H pan, R redline, E text, F strike & write, K callout, W strike row, T strike text, L line, A arrow, D length, P polyline, B box, O circle, X cross out — and the reference kit sits on Shift: Shift+O lasso, Shift+T text select, Shift+E eraser, Shift+S snapshot, Shift+L laser, Shift+F the property brush. Shift+D is day and night, M the marks list, / find, ? this page, and Escape puts down whatever is in your hand. Ctrl+Z / Ctrl+Y undo and redo everything, including a whole stamp run as one step. Ctrl+C, Ctrl+X and Ctrl+V work on marks the same way — including with the caret sitting in a mark, as long as no text is selected — and a paste lands where the pointer is, selected and ready to drag, rather than following the mouse until you click. Layering: ⤒ and ⤓ on either bar send marks to the front or the back, and a group moves as one layer, keeping its own internal order.
  • Marks are saved to this browser as you work, with version backups, so a refresh or a crash does not lose them. 💾 Save forces one; the tick above the ribbon says when it last happened.
  • REDLINE MOUNT ANALYSIS (Ctrl+E) exports. The original pages stay exactly as they were — vector, searchable, untouched — and every mark becomes a real PDF annotation: movable in Acrobat or Bluebeam, and re-editable here if you open the exported file again.
  • Other keys: / find, M the redlines drawer, D day / night. ⧉ Organize pages leaves a page out of the export. ▤ Redline report lists what was marked, page by page, to paste into an email.
